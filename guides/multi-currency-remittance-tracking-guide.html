Remittances

How to Track Money You Send Home Without Losing It in "Miscellaneous"

Bramora Labs · Updated August 26, 2026 · 5 min read
Short answer

Give remittances their own dedicated category — not "Transfers," not "Other" — and log each transfer in both the currency you sent and, if you track it, the amount received. That turns your biggest recurring expense from an invisible lump sum into a line item you can actually plan around.

Why this gets lost in most budgets

Most budgeting apps ship with generic categories — Food, Transport, Shopping, Bills — designed for someone spending entirely in one country. Money sent abroad doesn't fit cleanly into any of them, so it either gets dumped into "Other," recorded as a bank transfer with no category at all, or skipped entirely because it happened through a remittance app rather than a card swipe. For a lot of expats, this is the second-largest expense after rent — and it's the one most likely to be invisible in their own budget.

A simple system

  1. Create a dedicated "Remittance" category. Don't fold it into "Transfers" or "Family" — a category of its own means it shows up as its own slice when you look at where your month went.
  2. Log the amount you actually sent, in the currency you sent it. If you wired AED 2,000, record AED 2,000 — not an estimate of what it's worth in the destination currency, which shifts with the exchange rate anyway.
  3. Set a monthly or recurring entry if the amount is consistent. If you send roughly the same amount home every month, a recurring transaction means you don't have to remember to log it — and your budget accounts for it before the month starts, not after.
  4. Review it against your income, not in isolation. The useful number isn't "how much did I send" — it's "what percentage of what I earned did I send," tracked over a few months, so you can see if it's trending up or down.

Why the currency you send in matters

If your app forces every transaction into a single base currency, a remittance sent in AED gets silently converted using whatever exchange rate the app applies that day — which may not match the rate your remittance provider actually gave you. Over a year, that drift can make your "amount sent" numbers meaningfully wrong. Recording the transaction in the currency you actually paid in avoids that entirely.
